Problem statement

What happened inside Enron’s email network as the company collapsed?

  • Enron, a top-10 US company, went bankrupt in December 2001 after an accounting scandal.
  • US regulators (FERC) made the internal emails of about 150 employees public — one of the very few real corporate communication datasets.
  • The raw data is noisy (duplicates, aliases, bad timestamps) and sensitive (real people’s names).
How can we turn this raw email metadata into an interactive Tableau dashboard that shows how communication volume, communities and key people changed before, during and after the crisis — while protecting the employees’ privacy?

Target objectives

Ten objectives

  1. Acquire a real, publicly released dataset and document its source
  2. Clean it: remove duplicate copies, merge email aliases, check timestamps
  3. Model email traffic as a social network graph (strong vs weak ties)
  4. Test whether the graph shows social-network locality (Unit 5)
  5. Find key people and brokers with centrality measures
  6. Detect communities with Girvan–Newman and cross-check with Louvain
  7. Compare pre-crisis, crisis and post-bankruptcy communication
  8. Build an interactive Tableau dashboard using sound visualisation principles
  9. Compare Tableau Creator and Viewer licences for this dashboard
  10. Assess privacy, security and ethics; apply privacy-by-design (Unit 6)

Visualisation principles

Design choices and why we made them

Overview → detail

KPIs first, then trends, then filters and tooltips (Shneiderman’s mantra).

Reading order

Most important view top-left, supporting views below and right (Z-pattern).

Colour has one job

Same period colours everywhere; one-hue scale for amounts; colour-blind-checked palette for communities.

Right chart for the task

Lines for time, sorted bars for ranking, heat-map for a matrix, node-link for structure; no pie charts.

Less non-data ink

Meaningless graph axes and gridlines hidden; titles state what the chart shows.

Context & honesty

Key-events table for context; months with too few people filtered out of the locality chart.

Findings 1

The crisis tripled email traffic and pulled in the executives

163 → 541 deliveries a week (pre-crisis → crisis); the peak month was October 2001, when the $618M loss was announced.

Share of traffic involving an executive: 17% → 22% → 24%.

Broadcast emails (5+ recipients): 3.3% → 5.6% as news had to reach many people at once.

Findings 2

The informal network does not follow the org chart

  • 7 communities line up with real business units (leadership, gas pipeline, trading desks).
  • Two of the top four brokers have no job title in the data — informal brokers matter.
  • In the crisis, the top three brokers were all executives.

Unit 6 · Privacy, security & ethics

“But the data is already public” is not enough

Issues in this dataset

  • Consent: employees never agreed to have their email published; some messages were later removed at their request.
  • Secondary reuse: data released for a legal investigation is reused for research and teaching.
  • Re-identification: names, titles and network position identify people even when names are hidden.
  • Metadata reveals a lot: who-whom-when exposes hierarchy, cliques and work patterns.
  • Emerging risk: Enron emails are part of public AI training data, where models can leak addresses.

What we did

  • Metadata only — no message bodies, no topic labels, no email addresses.
  • Real names only for 10 company officers; everyone else is a role pseudonym (e.g. VP-03).
  • Job titles reduced to 7 coarse role groups to lower re-identification risk.
  • On Tableau Public: turn off workbook and data download; in an organisation, use Viewer roles and least privilege.
  • Reported data-quality limits openly (clock drift, missing titles).
Conclusion

What the dashboard shows

  • Cleaning mattered: 73% of raw records were duplicates or artefacts, and a timestamp drift would have produced a false finding.
  • The email graph passes the Unit 5 locality test (0.42 vs 0.16) and splits into 7 meaningful communities.
  • In the crisis, traffic rose 3.3× and shifted towards executives, who also became the main brokers.
  • Useful insight came from metadata alone, which is exactly why privacy-by-design was needed.
